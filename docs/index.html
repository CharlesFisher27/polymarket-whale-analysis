Do Whales Move Prediction Markets?

A computational event study of large-position holders and price impact on Polymarket — 503,662 comment–price observations across 183 markets spanning elections, geopolitics, crypto, and more.

The Question

Prediction markets like Polymarket aggregate beliefs about future events through real-money trading. Participants with large positions — whales — can also post public comments visible to all traders. Do these comments move prices? Do they move prices more in smaller, thinner markets? And do large holders use comments strategically to inflate or deflate prices before trading against the crowd?

These questions sit at the intersection of market microstructure, behavioral finance, and computational social science. If whale comments causally move prices, prediction markets aggregate information less efficiently than assumed.

Finding 1
Whale comment periods have larger price moves

Mean |Δp| = 0.21 after a whale comment vs 0.10 for retail — 2.2× larger. But this gap grows with market size, pointing to event-driven confounding rather than manipulation.

Finding 2
Gap grows with market size — opposite of manipulation

The whale–retail gap is largest in the biggest markets (elections, geopolitics). Thin-order-book manipulation would predict the opposite. Whales comment when news breaks in high-volume markets.

Finding 3
No evidence of "talking their book"

Among 4,910 whale events with known holder direction, sentiment was consistent with position only 52.8% of the time — barely above the 50% random benchmark. NO holders post positive sentiment most often (22.5%), inconsistent with their position.

Finding 4
OLS interaction term: null

The whale × log(volume) interaction is not significant (p = 0.85), ruling out the herding hypothesis. Comment sentiment also fails to predict price direction (p = 0.21). The evidence points to confounding, not causation.

Data

Data were assembled from three Polymarket public REST APIs: the Gamma API (comments, market metadata), the CLOB API (hourly YES-token price series), and the Data API (per-market top-200 holder positions). Markets span September 2023 through June 2026 across elections, geopolitics, crypto, and politics.

Whale definition: Any commenter whose wallet appears in the market's top-200 holder list with a position ≥ $5,000 USD. Of 503,662 total events, 4,910 (1.0%) are whale events across 87 markets. Mean whale position: $83,039 (median: $34,186).

Starting from 2,677,233 raw comment events across 470 markets, three filters reduce the sample: (1) events with no matchable price observation within ±7 days are dropped; (2) events where the pre-comment price is <0.10 or >0.90 are removed (convergence filter); (3) markets with std(|Δp|) < 0.001 are dropped for having effectively constant price histories. Fed Policy markets are excluded as they show no meaningful whale activity. The final sample is 503,662 events from 183 markets.

Methods

  1. Event study windows. For each comment, find the nearest observed YES-token price strictly before the comment and the nearest at or after the comment, within a ±7-day window. price_change = price_after − price_before. Median gap: ~25 hours on each side, confirming the 7-day window is not binding for most events.
  2. Whale tagging. Cross-reference each commenter's wallet address against the top-200 holders list. A comment is a "whale event" if the commenter holds ≥ $5,000 in the market.
  3. VADER sentiment. Each comment body is scored using the VADER lexicon (Hutto & Gilbert 2014), producing a compound score ∈ [−1, +1].
  4. Pooled OLS (market fixed effects). DV = signed price change after each comment. Tests whether whale events predict larger moves, controlling for each market's baseline behavior.
  5. Cross-market regression. DV = difference in mean |Δp| between whale and retail events per market. IV = market volume. Tests whether the whale–retail gap is larger in smaller, thinner markets.
  6. Position-direction analysis. Classify each whale as YES or NO holder (from outcomeIndex in holder data). Test whether comments are sentiment-consistent with position, and whether prices moved in the whale's financial favor.

Discussion & Takeaways

Across 503,662 events from 183 markets, whale comment events are followed by a mean absolute price move of |Δp| = 0.21, compared to 0.10 for retail — approximately 2.2× larger. But Figure 3 shows this gap is biggest in the largest, most liquid markets, not the smallest. That is the opposite of what a market-moving manipulation story would predict. The real explanation: whales in the Trump Win and Harris Win markets comment on election nights and major debate days, when prices swing sharply for everyone. Retail comments are spread across all ordinary days when nothing much happens.

The most likely explanation is event-driven confounding: whales are active participants who comment when they sense something is happening. That same event moves prices. The whale's comment co-occurs with the price move but is not its cause. Consistent with this, the whale × log(volume) interaction in M3 is essentially zero (β = −0.001, p = 0.85), ruling out the herding hypothesis that whale comments are more influential in thinner markets.

Why manipulation is unlikely despite the price correlation: If whales were deliberately pumping prices, their comment sentiment should consistently align with their financial position. Among 4,910 whale events with known holder direction, sentiment was consistent only 52.8% of the time — barely above random (50%). NO holders (those short the YES outcome) actually post positive sentiment 57.9% of the time, inconsistent with pumping their own position. Independently, event-driven confounding argues against manipulation: prices move on news days whether or not a whale comments.

Limitations & Future Work

Key limitations: (1) event-driven confounding cannot be fully ruled out without exogenous variation in comment timing; (2) holder snapshots are taken at market close, not at comment time — position may have changed; (3) some markets had zero or very few whale commenters, limiting power. Future work should use minute-level price data, difference-in-differences designs, and API access to historical holder snapshots.

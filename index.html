Do Whales Move Prediction Markets?

The Question

Prediction markets like Polymarket aggregate beliefs about future events through real-money trading. Participants with large positions — whales — can also post public comments visible to all traders. Do these comments move prices? Do they move prices more in smaller, thinner markets? And do large holders use comments strategically to inflate or deflate prices before trading against the crowd?

These questions sit at the intersection of market microstructure, behavioral finance, and computational social science. If whale comments causally move prices, prediction markets aggregate information less efficiently than assumed.

Finding 1
Whale comment periods have larger price moves

Mean |Δp| = 0.23 after a whale comment vs 0.085 for retail — approximately 2.7× larger. But this is almost certainly because whales comment on election nights and Fed days when prices genuinely swing, not because their comments cause moves.

Finding 2
Gap is largest in the biggest markets

In Q5 markets (>$30M volume, e.g. Trump Win), whale events show 3× larger |Δp| than retail. In smaller markets, the gap is tiny. This pattern matches timing — not thin order books.

Finding 3
No evidence of "talking their book"

Among ~2,100 whale events where the commenter's position was known, sentiment was consistent with their holdings only 49.8% of the time — statistically indistinguishable from chance (50%). Whales do not appear to comment strategically to move prices in their favor.

Finding 4
Most likely: event-driven confounding

Whales comment when news breaks. News moves prices. The whale's comment co-occurs with the price move but doesn't cause it. Comments are a signal that something is happening, not the cause.

Data

Data were assembled from three Polymarket public REST APIs: the Gamma API (comments, market metadata), the CLOB API (hourly YES-token price series), and the Data API (per-wallet holder positions). Markets span 2024–2026 across elections, Fed policy decisions, geopolitics, crypto, and politics.

Whale definition: Any commenter with a net position ≥ $5,000 USD in the market, identified by cross-referencing commenter wallet addresses against the top-holders list.

Of the 279 markets collected, 140 have usable price data for the event study: 107 were excluded because their prices were near 0 or 1 the entire sample period (already-resolved markets), and 32 were excluded for having only one or two distinct price points (sparse price history that cannot support a meaningful before/after comparison). Methods

  1. Event study windows. For each comment, measure the YES-token probability in a 1-hour pre-window and a 2-hour post-window using forward-filled hourly CLOB price series. price_change = price_after − price_before.
  2. Whale tagging. Cross-reference each commenter's wallet address against the top-200 holders list. A comment is a "whale event" if the commenter holds ≥ $5,000 in the market.
  3. VADER sentiment. Each comment body is scored using the VADER lexicon (Hutto & Gilbert 2014), producing a compound score ∈ [−1, +1].
  4. Pooled OLS (market fixed effects). DV = signed price change. Tests directional effect while controlling for market-level baseline. HC3 heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors. Primary result is the unsigned absolute |Δp| comparison (Figure 3).
  5. Cross-market regression. Unit of analysis = market. DV = whale_effect (mean |Δp| whale − mean |Δp| retail). IV = log₁₀(market volume). Tests whether whale influence is stronger in thinner markets (Barberis herding prediction).
  6. Position-direction analysis. Classify each whale as YES or NO holder (from outcomeIndex in holder data). Test whether comments are sentiment-consistent with position, and whether prices moved in the whale's financial favor.

Discussion & Takeaways

Across 289,321 observations from 140 markets with usable price data, whale comment events are followed by a mean absolute price move of |Δp| = 0.23, compared to 0.085 for retail — approximately 2.7× larger. But Figure 3 shows this gap is biggest in the largest, most liquid markets, not the smallest. That is the opposite of what a market-moving manipulation story would predict. The real explanation: whales in the Trump Win ($1.5B) and Harris Win ($1B) markets comment on election nights and major debate days, when prices move dramatically for everyone. Retail comments are spread across all ordinary days when nothing much happens.

The most likely explanation is event-driven confounding: whales are active participants who comment when they sense something is happening. That same event moves prices. The whale's comment co-occurs with the price move but is not its cause.

Why manipulation is unlikely despite the price correlation: If whales were deliberately pumping prices, their comment sentiment should consistently align with their financial position. Among the ~2,100 whale events where we could identify the commenter's position, sentiment was consistent only 49.8% of the time — indistinguishable from random. Independently, event-driven confounding argues against manipulation: prices move on news days whether or not a whale comments.

Limitations & Future Work

Key limitations: (1) event-driven confounding cannot be fully ruled out without exogenous variation in comment timing; (2) holder snapshots are taken at market close, not at comment time — position may have changed; (3) some markets had zero or very few whale commenters, limiting power. Future work should use minute-level price data, difference-in-differences designs, and API access to historical holder snapshots.
